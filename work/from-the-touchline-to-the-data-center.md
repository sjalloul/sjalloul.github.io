Before Gillette was a place I worked, it was a name that went around the field.

If you grew up watching the World Cup, you know the ones — the advertising boards ringing the pitch, the names that scroll past behind every corner kick and every celebration. Gillette was always one of them. As a kid, it didn't register as a company so much as part of the furniture of the game itself: global, permanent, everywhere at once, the kind of name that seemed to belong to the whole world rather than to any particular building in any particular city. You don't think, watching from the couch, that behind a name like that is a campus, a payroll, a room full of servers. It's just there, the way the grass is there.

Years later I found out where the building was. It was in South Boston.

I was an undergraduate at Northeastern, and if you know Northeastern you know the co-op program — the thing that pulls you out of the classroom and drops you into a real job for months at a stretch, again and again, so that you graduate having actually done the work instead of only reading about it. Mine put me inside The Gillette Company. Not on a billboard. In the data center.

The reality behind the brand was a room full of machines that were not allowed to stop. I spent my days helping run the day-to-day of an enterprise built on hundreds of servers — north of four hundred Windows systems, a full spread of Windows NT, 2000, and 2003, and, at the leading edge, the first wave of VMware virtual machines that were just then starting to change how data centers got built. I wrote installation and rollout plans for enterprise software. I worked on disaster-recovery and business-resumption planning — the deeply unglamorous, deeply important discipline of making sure a global company could keep running if the worst happened. I helped consolidate and convert servers, chased down system problems and security issues, and tried to keep pace with where the industry was heading, which in those years meant watching virtualization quietly begin to eat everything.

None of that is the part of a company that ends up on a billboard. It was the part that let the billboard exist. And it was the first time I really understood the difference — that behind every brand you admire from a distance is a floor full of people keeping alive the systems that support operations no customer ever sees. The name goes around the pitch; someone has to keep the machine behind it running. For a while, I got to be one of those someones, at a company I'd watched my whole life.

Somewhere in the middle of it, the badge changed. Gillette — a piece of Boston, a company with more than a century of history in the city — was acquired by Procter & Gamble. I started at The Gillette Company and, without moving desks, became a P&G employee. As a young engineer it was a strange and useful thing to watch up close: how a name that had felt permanent since childhood could fold into something larger, how companies and whole industries move whether or not you're ready for them to. You learn early that nothing in this business is furniture. It all moves eventually.

The other thing I took from those years is harder to put on a résumé, and it came from the people — from the manager who ran that team in particular. Being an intern is mostly an exercise in hoping someone will trust you with something real. Mine did. I was handed actual responsibility, systems that mattered and work that had consequences, and I was expected to rise to it, with enough room to make mistakes and enough guidance to learn from them. A good boss at the right moment does something no curriculum can: they show you where the bar is by holding you to it. I don't think I fully appreciated at the time how much that shaped me. I do now.

What that co-op gave me wasn't a skill set, though the skills mattered and I still use their descendants every day. It was a grounding. It's where I learned that "systems supporting critical operations" is not a line on a job description — it's a responsibility, and the weight of it is the whole point. Almost everything I've done since has been some version of that same lesson: keep the important things running, plan honestly for the day they don't, and take the boring, essential work at least as seriously as the exciting kind. I can trace a fairly straight line from that server room in South Boston to most of what came after.

I still catch the Gillette boards during a match sometimes, scrolling around the pitch the way they did when I was a kid. It lands differently now. I know what's behind the name — not the myth, but the machine, and the people who keep it alive. For a few formative years, I was lucky enough to be one of them. I'm grateful I started there.
